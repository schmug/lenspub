# Legal Considerations

**Status:** Draft v0.1 · **Date:** 2026-07-09 · **License:** CC-BY 4.0

*This document is informative and non-normative. It surveys the legal context in which LensPub implementations operate, and explains where the protocol's design decisions are also legally conservative choices. It is written by and for engineers, not lawyers; nothing in it is legal advice (see Section 7). Implementers should obtain advice for their own jurisdiction and circumstances.*

## 1. Annotating third-party content

LensPub's core act — overlaying annotations on someone else's page — has a legal shape worth stating plainly. A Lens Engine operates inside the user's own user agent, after rendering, on content the user has lawfully retrieved for their own reading ([ADR-0008](../adr/0008-interpretation-is-overlay-stage.md)). It does not copy the page to a server, does not republish it, does not create or distribute a modified version, and does not alter what any other person sees. In this respect it belongs to a long, generally accepted family of user-agent transformations: screen readers, reader modes, translation, user style sheets, and content blockers. The overlay exists transiently on the user's device for the user's own consumption; there is no distribution of a derivative work, because there is no distribution.

That is the design argument. It is discussed here rather than asserted as settled law, because the surrounding case law is genuinely unsettled and the disputes that exist are cautionary. In *ML Genius Holdings v. Google* (2d Cir. 2022), Genius — itself an annotation platform — pursued Google for allegedly copying its lyric transcriptions; the courts held Genius's contract claims preempted by copyright, and the dispute illustrates how contested rights in *layers over other people's content* can become once those layers are collected and redistributed at scale. The *hiQ Labs v. LinkedIn* litigation (9th Cir. 2019, 2022) established that scraping public data is likely not a federal computer-crime violation, yet hiQ ultimately lost on contract grounds — a reminder that terms of service remain a live constraint on automated access even where criminal statutes do not reach. Neither case involved a user-agent-side overlay, and that is the point: the legal danger zone in this family of disputes is server-side collection and redistribution, which LensPub's architecture never performs.

History supplies the same caution from the publisher-relations side. Third Voice (1999–2001), which let users attach public notes to any website via a proxy-visible layer, was denounced by publishers as defacement and did not survive; Genius's proxy-based web annotator drew similar objections. LensPub's constitution requires that original content remain unchanged — interpretation is *layered, never substituted*, and engines never rewrite, obscure by default, or reorder the underlying page. This is first an ethical commitment to author intent, but it is also the legally conservative posture: an overlay that visibly adds the user's own interpretive layer, attributable to the user's lens, is categorically different from a tool that alters or misrepresents what a publisher said. Implementers should preserve this property strictly; the specification gives them no license to do otherwise.

## 2. Primary-source expansion, copyright, and paywalls

The `primary-source` interpretation kind expands references toward original sources. The conservative pattern, and the one the specification's examples follow, is: *link out* to the source; when quoting, excerpt only what fits within ordinary quotation norms (fair use, fair dealing, and the quotation exceptions of Berne Convention jurisdictions — short, attributed, purposive excerpts); and never bulk-reproduce a source's content inside an overlay. An Overlay Annotation's `basis` structure is built for attribution — a URI plus a description — and engines should treat it that way rather than as a container for wholesale copied text.

Paywalls deserve their own sentence. A Lens Engine interprets content the user can already see; it must not become a paywall-circumvention tool, and implementers should not fetch, cache, or surface paywalled source text the user has not lawfully accessed. Beyond being corrosive to publisher relations, circumventing technical protection measures can violate anti-circumvention law (for example, 17 U.S.C. § 1201 in the United States and Article 6 of the EU InfoSoc Directive). Linking to a paywalled source is always acceptable; extracting its text is not the engine's job.

## 3. Terms-of-service friction for engines

Implementers face two distinct terms-of-service surfaces. First, websites: some publishers' terms purport to restrict automated processing of their pages. A local Lens Engine processing a page the user is currently reading, for that user only, is much closer to a screen reader than to a scraper — it collects nothing, retains only device-local state, and republishes nothing — but implementers distributing engines commercially should evaluate this posture rather than assume it. Second, model providers: when a user opts into remote inference ([ADR-0005](../adr/0005-local-only-default.md)), page excerpts flow to a hosted model under that provider's terms, including their data-use and retention policies. Engines are required to make this trust-boundary crossing visible; implementers should additionally read the provider terms they are routing users' reading into, and say plainly in their own documentation what the provider may do with the data. Browser-extension store policies (Chrome Web Store, Firefox Add-ons) impose a third, practical layer of review on the reference-implementation form factor.

## 4. Evidence indicators and defamation-adjacent care

An overlay that says something negative about a named source is the closest LensPub comes to defamation exposure, and the epistemic stance of [ADR-0007](../adr/0007-epistemic-stance.md) is legal risk reduction as well as epistemology. Engines surface *checkable signals with their basis shown* — this article has no citations, this image carries no Content Credentials, a counterpoint exists at this attributed source — and are forbidden to emit verdicts of the form "this is false" or "this source lies." Statements of verifiable fact, accurately made and sourced, and clearly framed evaluative opinions are the defensible categories; unsourced machine-generated assertions of fact about identifiable parties are the dangerous one, and the schema's requirement of a `basis` on every evidence-class annotation is designed to keep engines out of it. Lens authors should observe the same discipline in manifest text: the `note` field on a distrusted source is carried into reasoning traces and, in a Published Lens, becomes a statement to the world — keep it factual ("has issued repeated corrections without notice") or plainly opinion, not accusation. Publishers of Expert and Organization Lenses, whose distrust lists reach many users, carry proportionally more of this responsibility.

## 5. Name and trademark hygiene

"LensPub" is a provisional working name, as the namespace `https://lenspub.org/ns/` is provisional. "Lens" is a crowded term: among others, an existing decentralized social protocol uses the name "Lens Protocol," and several consumer products use "Lens" as a brand. LensPub is unaffiliated with all of them. Before permanent namespace and media-type registration (see the [roadmap](roadmap.md)), the project should run a proper trademark clearance search, register the domain and name it intends to keep, and — if collision risk proves real — rename while the cost is still low. Implementations should avoid implying endorsement by the LensPub project in their own naming ("built on LensPub" rather than "LensPub Browser") until a trademark policy exists.

## 6. Why the conservative design is the design

A recurring pattern in this document is worth making explicit: the constitution's ethical commitments and the legally cautious positions coincide. Layered-not-substituted overlay respects author intent *and* avoids derivative-work and misrepresentation theories. Local-first processing respects privacy *and* avoids the collection and redistribution that drove the scraping cases. Surfacing sourced signals instead of verdicts respects epistemic humility *and* avoids defamation exposure. Linking out instead of reproducing respects publishers *and* copyright. This alignment is not accidental, and implementers who deviate from the constitution's commitments lose both protections at once.

## 7. Disclaimer

This document provides context, not legal advice. It is not exhaustive, it simplifies contested doctrine, it is weighted toward United States and European Union law, and it will age. No attorney-client relationship is created by reading it. Implementers and lens publishers should consult qualified counsel in their own jurisdictions before making decisions that depend on the questions discussed here.
