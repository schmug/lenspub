# Vision

**Status:** Draft v0.1 · **Date:** 2026-07-09 · **License:** CC-BY 4.0

This is a vision-level document; it contains no normative requirements. Precise definitions live in the [Glossary](../GLOSSARY.md), the [specifications](../spec/lenspub-protocol.md), and the [Architecture Decision Records](../adr/README.md).

## Every collapse in the cost of creation forces a new quality gate

When a technology makes information dramatically cheaper to produce, the volume produced quickly outruns any individual's capacity to evaluate it. Societies respond, each time, by inventing an institution to decide what deserves attention. The printing press made books cheap, and the flood of print gave rise to publishers and editors — professional gates between manuscript and reader. The web made publishing free, and billions of pages gave rise to search engines — algorithmic gates between query and result. Social media made broadcasting free, and the infinite feed gave rise to platform recommendation algorithms — personalized gates between everything posted and the finite scroll of one person's day.

The pattern has two halves, and both matter. Each gate genuinely solved the volume problem of its era. But each gate also concentrated a new kind of power: whoever operates it decides, in practice, what a person effectively reads — and each successive gate has been operated further from the reader, on terms the reader can neither inspect nor carry away.

## The gate that has not been built

Generative AI is the fourth collapse, and it is steeper than the previous three. Plausible prose, images, audio, and video can now be produced at near-zero marginal cost, at industrial scale, tuned to be persuasive. The question every reader faces on every page — *is this worth my attention, and what should I make of it?* — has never applied to more items or been harder to answer unaided. The [problem statement](problem-statement.md) treats this in depth; the short version is that the quality gate for the generative-AI era does not yet exist.

The default trajectory is that platforms will build it, again: ranking, filtering, and now machine-written summaries, operated by the same engagement-funded machinery that built the last gate, with a large model added to the loop. If that happens, the most consequential filter in a person's intellectual life will be owned, tuned, and monetized by someone else, invisibly.

LensPub's thesis is that the next quality gate should belong to the individual, not the platform — not because platforms are uniquely malicious, but because deciding what information is valuable *to you* is too close to the core of a person's thinking to be delegated without inspection, correction, or exit. Every person should be able to own, inspect, modify, version, and share their own interpretation layer.

## Interpretation: a layer the reader owns

Conceptually, the web a user experiences is shaped by transport, content, presentation, and behavior. LensPub adds a further stage: **interpretation** — a layer of user-controlled meaning above the rendered page. Through it, content the reader has chosen to view arrives enriched with Overlay Annotations: contextual notes, summaries at the reader's preferred depth, evidence indicators pointing at checkable provenance, sourced counterpoints, primary-source expansions — and, attached to every one, a Reasoning Trace explaining *why* it appears. The lens metaphor is chosen with care. A lens does not alter the object; it changes how the object is seen. It can be taken off, examined, handed to a friend, or reground to a new prescription. None of that is true of a recommendation algorithm.

Stated precisely, interpretation is not a new layer of the network stack. It is a **user-agent-side, post-render overlay stage**: a Lens Engine executes on the user's device or user agent, after content, presentation, and behavior have done their work, and operates on the rendered document — the DOM and the accessibility tree — producing overlays that sit visually and semantically above the content without mutating it. In the browser proof of concept this is a content-script stage; the definition generalizes to any user agent that exposes a rendered-content tree, including e-readers and feed clients. The evocative word "layer" survives in documents like this one; the exact operating point is fixed in [ADR-0008](../adr/0008-interpretation-is-overlay-stage.md) and the [Architecture specification](../architecture/architecture.md).

The portable heart of the system is the **Lens Manifest**: a declarative, model-agnostic policy document expressing what the user wants emphasized, whom they trust and distrust, what provenance they want surfaced, and how readily their lens may evolve. It is never model weights and never a prompt ([ADR-0001](../adr/0001-manifest-is-declarative-policy.md)); any conforming Lens Engine compiles the same manifest into its own machinery. The overlay itself is a profile of the [W3C Web Annotation Data Model](https://www.w3.org/TR/annotation-model/) ([ADR-0002](../adr/0002-profile-web-annotation.md)): the original content remains unchanged, and interpretation is layered rather than substituted.

## Six properties

The vision commits the interpretation layer to six properties, each load-bearing.

**It operates locally whenever possible.** Interpretation runs on the user's device by default, with no network dependency. Any use of a remote model is an explicit, per-domain, revocable opt-in, and every crossing of that trust boundary is visible ([ADR-0005](../adr/0005-local-only-default.md)). The layer that watches how you read must, by construction, report to no one.

**It transparently explains its decisions.** The manifest is a human-readable document, not an opaque learned artifact, and every Overlay Annotation carries a Reasoning Trace naming the manifest rule, priority, or signal that produced it. There is no "the algorithm decided" — there is a specific line in a document you own.

**It never silently changes.** This promise is made honestly, in two parts ([ADR-0004](../adr/0004-reproducibility-envelope.md)). The Lens Manifest is guaranteed stable: it changes only through the proposal workflow, and every version is immutable, content-addressed, and diffable. Interpretation *output*, when produced by large models, cannot be guaranteed identical over time — so instead of overclaiming, every Interpretation Result carries a Reproducibility Envelope recording the engine, model, parameters, and prompt template used. The policy never changes silently; the machinery that renders it is instrumented so its changes are never invisible.

**It evolves only under user-defined policies.** Learning is never silent. A lens adapts through Lens Change Proposals — generated from explicit feedback, evaluated, and reviewed — under one of five Adaptation Policies, from Locked to Explorer, defined as points on explicit protocol parameters ([ADR-0010](../adr/0010-adaptation-policies-parameterized.md)) and settable per Domain Scope. A reader can keep their politics lens frozen while letting their cooking lens roam.

**It is portable between devices and applications.** The manifest is a document with a schema, not an account with a vendor. It moves between browsers, devices, and engines; what is guaranteed to travel is the *policy*, while interpretation richness varies with the engine's declared capability tier.

**It can be versioned, diffed, exported, and shared.** Every accepted change produces a new Lens Version; a Lens Diff can compare a user with their past self, with a friend, or with a subscribed Published Lens. Sharing rests on Signed Manifests using [W3C Verifiable Credentials and DIDs](https://www.w3.org/TR/vc-data-integrity/) ([ADR-0003](../adr/0003-adopt-vc2-dids-for-trust.md)), and it is safe by construction: the shareable object is the history-free manifest core, which never contained browsing history ([ADR-0006](../adr/0006-history-free-shareable-core.md)).

## Intellectual lineage

LensPub is not a bolt from the blue; it sits at the confluence of several existing lines of thought, treated fully in [Relationship to Prior Art](prior-art.md).

The most direct ancestor is the **middleware** proposal of Francis Fukuyama and colleagues in the Stanford Working Group on Platform Scale ("How to Save Democracy from Technology," *Foreign Affairs*, 2021): let competing third-party services take over curation from dominant platforms, so that no single ranking function governs public attention. LensPub pushes middleware one step further — from a market of curation *services* between user and platform, to a curation *policy document* in the user's own possession, executable by any engine. Mike Masnick's "[Protocols, Not Platforms](https://knightcolumbia.org/content/protocols-not-platforms-a-technological-approach-to-free-speech)" (Knight First Amendment Institute, 2019) supplies the structural argument LensPub adopts wholesale: durable solutions to curation problems come from open protocols with many implementations, not from better-behaved platforms. And Bluesky's composable moderation on the AT Protocol — stackable, user-selected labeling services — is a running existence proof that user-chosen interpretation can operate at scale. LensPub generalizes the idea from labels inside one social network to interpretation across the whole web, and from subscribed services to portable, inspectable documents.

A second, quieter lineage is philosophical. Work on epistemic autonomy and dependence (Hardwig's "Epistemic Dependence," 1985; Matheson and Lougheed, eds., *Epistemic Autonomy*, 2022) examines when relying on others to filter knowledge is rational and when it corrodes the ability to think for oneself; the attention-economy critique (James Williams, *Stand Out of Our Light*, 2018) argues that systems optimizing our attention against our own goals are a distinct harm deserving distinct defenses. LensPub is an engineering answer to the question this literature poses: what would a person's information filter look like as an instrument of their own agency rather than a site of someone else's?

## If LensPub succeeds

Picture a technologist named Avery a few years from now. Avery's lens — "avery-daily" — runs locally in whatever browser and devices Avery uses this year. Reading an unfamiliar site, Avery sees quiet evidence indicators noting missing provenance credentials and corroborating primary sources, each expandable to show its basis and reasoning. During an outbreak Avery subscribes to an epidemiologist's Expert Lens, scoped to health topics only, and reads exactly what that lens contributes before adopting any of it. When Avery's own lens proposes learning a new preference, the proposal arrives as a reviewable diff; twice a year Avery diffs the current lens against January's and sees, in plain language, how their filter has changed. Nothing about Avery's reading history has ever left the device.

Around that individual picture, an ecosystem: independent Lens Engines competing on interpretation quality under a common manifest format, the way browsers compete under common web standards; researchers, journalists, and civic organizations publishing signed lenses anyone can inspect before trusting; and platform recommendation still existing — LensPub does not abolish feeds — but no longer the *only* gate, because every reader carries their own. The measure of success is structural: the question "why am I seeing this?" always has an answer, the answer lives in a document the reader owns, and leaving any vendor never means leaving your judgment behind.

## What LensPub does not promise

Honesty about limits is part of the vision. LensPub is **not a truth engine** ([ADR-0007](../adr/0007-epistemic-stance.md)): a Lens Engine surfaces verifiable signals — provenance, citations, source identity, attributed counterpoints — and always shows their basis, but it never issues verdicts of the form "this is true" or "this is false." A world of LensPub users is a world with better instruments for judgment, not a world relieved of judging. It is **not a recommendation algorithm**: it ranks interpretations of content the user has already chosen to view, under a policy the user can read — it does not select what exists in anyone's feed. It does **not reduce the supply** of synthetic content; it is a demand-side gate. It does not promise an **identical experience everywhere**: the manifest is portable, but engines of different capability tiers will honor it with different richness, and the protocol says so plainly. And it is not effortless: ownership carries stewardship. Adaptation Policies, sensible defaults, and subscriptions make that stewardship light — but the reader who wants no hand at all on their own filter is asking for exactly the arrangement LensPub was built to replace.

What LensPub does promise is narrower and, we believe, keepable: an open, vendor-neutral protocol under which your interpretation layer is yours — local by default, explained on demand, changed only with your consent, and portable for life.
